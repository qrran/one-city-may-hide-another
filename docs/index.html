The source poem

One Train
May Hide
Another.

Kenneth Koch

Selected opening lines · English original

In a poem, one line may hide another line,
As at a crossing, one train may hide another train.

— Kenneth Koch, One Train May Hide Another

Koch begins with a simple warning at a railway crossing: what is immediately visible may conceal something else. He carries that observation into the act of reading, then into our relationships and the ways we perceive the world.

The poem invites us to pause before assuming we have seen everything. That pause becomes the organizing idea of the film and the interactive city studies on this site.

Director’s note

A pause in
the line.

Dan Lin

In Kenneth Koch’s One Train May Hide Another, the phrase “at least” breaks across two lines. I have to wait for the next line to finish even that small thought. I wanted to give a New York commute that same extra beat.

The spoon taps twice on both visits to the coffee cart. The first time through, I’m watching the crossing light. On the second pass I can hear what the vendor is doing. At the brass door, I’ve already called it luck by the time I notice someone’s hand.

Whose morning is it?

For the student, the coffee cart is one stop on the way to class. For the vendor, that corner is the workday. When the light changes, the student can leave. The vendor is still there, making the next cup. What looks like one morning from the train window holds two very different ways of spending an hour.

First pass · 0:28Second pass · 1:25

Later in the poem, Koch writes, “One injustice may hide another.” That line makes me imagine a glass lobby on a cold New York morning. An employee’s badge opens the door. Someone without a place to sleep might look at that same lobby and see somewhere warm to sit. They can stand on the same block and have a different city available to them.

The rainy Brooklyn coffee shop asks a similar question in a single place. What feels like a brief stop for one person may be the center of another person's day. I wanted the act of exploring the scene to echo the act of rereading Koch: one view is never the whole story.

The film never tells me who held the brass door or whether they can go inside. I like leaving that question open. The second look changes what I notice, even when it gives me no right to fill in the rest of a stranger’s life.
